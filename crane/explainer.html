Crane Sway

An overhead crane moves a load hanging on a rope. Every time the bridge speeds up or brakes, the load swings like a pendulum, and the operator has to wait for it to settle before setting it down. Anti-sway systems shape the motion so the swing cancels, but they need a model of the pendulum: rope length, and usually an operator-entered sling length. When the rigging is longer than entered, or a light load on long slings swings like a double pendulum, the model is wrong and the swing comes back. We keep the industry's shaper and add TabPFN on top: before every move, it predicts the residual swing and the settle time of each candidate (speed, shaper tuning), from this crane's own recent moves, and picks the fastest one that keeps the load clear of the racks. No training loop: new rows in the table are the adaptation. On a real lab crane, TabPFN cut its 1-second swing error 3× after watching an unseen load for 5 s (section 6), and the same in a full-size MuJoCo crane (6b).

2 · Why loads swing

Swing is set by acceleration and timing, not by speed

When the bridge accelerates at a, the load lags behind by an angle of about a/g. It then swings around that angle. When the acceleration stops, it keeps swinging with an amplitude that depends on how long the push lasted compared with the swing period. Push for exactly one period and the swing cancels itself. Input shaping uses this effect. Move the sliders: the dips in the curve are the timings that leave no swing.

The curves are simulated live: a single push of length tₐ, then coasting, measuring the largest load offset afterwards. With a heavy load on a short sling (R large, L₂ small), mode 2 barely matters and the formula fits. Drag the sling to 2.6 m and the payload ratio to 1.6, as for light pallets on long slings. The shaper tuned for an entered 0.5 m sling now misses its period, and the second mode adds swing no single-mode shaper removes.

4 · How each move is decided

28 questions, one batched call, the fastest safe answer

Step 1 · sense

Lift and measure

Read rope length, payload and the lift-off sway period. No sling length is entered.

Step 2 · one batched call

Ask 28 questions

One row per candidate: 7 speeds × 4 shaper tunings, outcomes blank. TabPFN returns a distribution of residual swing and settle time for each.

Step 3 · choose

Fastest under the risk limit

Expected cycle = move time + predicted settle + P(collision) × 20 s. Keep candidates with P(amp > 0.45 m) ≤ 1% and take the lowest. If none qualify, fall back to the slowest speed.

Step 4 · log

Add the row

After set-down, the actual swing and settle time go into the table. The next query reads it. Adapting means adding rows; nothing is retrained.

Last decision

Chosen speed over the shift

Each dot is one move of the teal lane. The dot's colour shows the sling the shaper assumed: light teal 0.5 m to dark 3.5 m. Red marks a collision. Shaded bands are the scenario phases.

5 · Calibration

Does a 90% range hold the swing 90% of the time?

The decision rule trusts the predicted spread, not only the median. If the spread is too narrow, the crane collides more often than it promised. If it is too wide, the crane runs slower than it needs to. So we check coverage, the share of moves whose actual swing landed inside the predicted 90% range. Coverage should be close to 0.90.

// what we compute (here live, in Python on held-out jobs) coverage₉₀ = (1/n) Σ 1[ q₀.₀₅ ≤ yᵢ ≤ q₀.₉₅ ] target ≈ 0.90 pinball loss (quantile q): ρ_q(y, ŷ) = max( q(y−ŷ), (q−1)(y−ŷ) ) PIT uniformity: uᵢ = F̂ᵢ(yᵢ) should look uniform on [0,1] Brier (collision): (1/n) Σ (p̂ᵢ − 1[hitᵢ])² ECE: |observed − predicted| rate, averaged over bins of p̂

An honest caveat: the in-browser stand-in errs on the wide side. On 120 held-out mixed-shift jobs, its 50% range held 88% of outcomes and its 90% range held 99%. That makes it cautious: safe, but slower than needed. TabPFN outputs a full predictive distribution (bar distribution, output_type="quantiles"), and its real calibration is something we measure, not assume. On the real lab crane it measured too tight on the new payload and on hoisting (30–72% inside the 90% range), and close to 90% after 5 s of the new load's rows (section 6).

6 · Real lab data

On a real crane, the textbook pendulum is wrong in every run

I searched for public crane sway datasets and checked each one. One fits: a lab tower crane from Nazarbayev University, released on Mendeley Data under CC BY 4.0. It has 33 runs of 99 s each at 100 Hz. They cover trolley moves at 8 cable lengths (0.1–0.8 m), three payload shapes and hoisting. In every trolley run the measured swing is slower than 2π√(L/g) with the logged cable length. It behaves as if the rope were 12–17 cm longer, and up to 28 cm longer with a beam payload. The logged columns can't explain that offset; the crane's own swing data can.

6b · MuJoCo demo

We built an industrial overhead crane in MuJoCo: a trolley on a rail, a rope to a hook, slings to a beam, and racks beside two set-down bays. Then we gave it a load it had never seen: a light 400 kg, 2.4 m beam on 2.6 m slings, while the anti-sway is still set for 0.5 m. Every 0.1 s, TabPFN predicts where the load will be 1 s later. 7 · Problem statement and training

Every parameter, formula and choice behind the experiment

This section is the specification for the Python version: what is simulated, what goes into each row, how data is split, how TabPFN is called, what it is compared with, and what could make the result wrong.

7.1Problem statement

For each move i, the crane observes a context xi (rope length, payload, measured sway period) and must choose an action u = (speed fraction, shaper tuning). We want the fastest cycle that keeps the collision risk under ε:

u*ᵢ = argmin_u T_move(u) + E[ T_settle | xᵢ, u ] + P(hit | xᵢ, u) · T_penalty subject to P( amp > c | xᵢ, u ) ≤ ε T_move(u) = D / v + v / a + Δ_ZV // trapezoid move + shaper delay, computed exactly Δ_ZV = π √((L₁ + sling_assumed) / g) c = 0.45 m clearance ε = 1% T_penalty = 20 s D = 10 m

TabPFN supplies the two unknowns: the predictive distribution of amp and of settle given (x, u). Everything else is computed exactly. This is the Prior Labs track question, "where is it going and what happens next", posed for one move of one load.

7.3Data: pre-existing logs, then the crane's own

  1. Pre-existing fleet logs (the "expert" data). 90 commissioning moves on short slings (L₂ = 0.5 m, L₁ 3.2–4.8 m, 2–3 t), with random speeds and tunings. That's the range a site's commissioning engineer would cover. This is the same role the pretrained flybody policy plays for experiment C on that branch. In Python we also log a shaper expert (ZV tuned on T_meas) driving thousands of moves, which is the imitation-learning dataset.
  2. Scenario jobs. Four phases of 400 jobs each, from a fixed random seed, identical for all strategies. Phase 0 has coils on short slings. Phase 1 is a high lift (L₁ ≈ 7 m). Phase 2 is light pallets on long slings (L₂ ≈ 2.6 m, R ≈ 1.6). Phase 3 is a mixed shift (everything random).
  3. Real lab crane data (section 6). 33 runs from a Nazarbayev University lab tower crane (CC BY 4.0). It's used to validate the forward model on real swing and to set realistic offsets in the sim. For example, the measured period fits L + 0.12…0.17 m, not L.
  4. One row per move, not per time step, because the decision is made once per move. For the secondary forward-prediction experiment (A, below) we also log rows at 10 Hz during motion.
rows for the decision model: n_ctx = 90 fleet + one per completed move rows for forward prediction: 10 Hz × (move + settle) ≈ 200–400 rows per move → subsample

7.4Features: why summaries beat raw values

Our first stand-in used raw rope length. It could not tell that a 7 m rope with a correctly tuned shaper is the same situation as the 4 m rope in the fleet logs. So it stayed at 40–50% speed on every new condition. Replacing raw lengths with tune_err, the shaper's period error relative to the measured period, made those rows look alike. The same model then ran at full speed where physics allowed it and slowed only where it had to. This is Prior Labs' lesson 3, features before raw data, measured on our own sim.

T_meas = 2π / ω₁(L₁, L₂, R) · (1 + 0.03 ε), ε ~ N(0,1) // what the sensor sees ln_R = ln(m₂ / m₁) v_frac ∈ {0.4, 0.5, …, 1.0} tune_err = 2π √((L₁ + sling_assumed)/g) / T_meas − 1 // 0 = shaper period matches the measured one

Missing values stay NaN. TabPFN handles NaN natively (its cell embedding has a NaN flag), so there's no need to impute. The stand-in simply skips a missing feature when comparing rows.

Time budget. The decision happens once per move, during lift-off, about 1–2 s before travel starts. A 22–64 ms query fits about 20× inside that window. Rubric lesson 1 (loop rate) is met by design rather than by tuning.

Usage budget. Cost and speed grow with (context rows + query rows) × features: here (1,000 + 28) × 4 ≈ 4k cells per call. Run locally, that's zero API usage. Through the API, each call is charged at least 10k tokens, with 60 calls per minute and about 5M tokens per day by default. That allows about 500 calls a day, enough for offline experiments but not a live crane. So the API is for batch experiments only.

7.7The in-browser stand-in, exactly

This is the model the teal lane uses on this page. It is a kernel-weighted average over the logged rows, plus a weak prior. It's simple on purpose, so nobody mistakes it for TabPFN.

scaled distance d²(x, r) = Σⱼ ((xⱼ − rⱼ)/sⱼ)², s = [1.0 s, 0.7, 0.12, 0.08] (missing → +1) weight wᵣ = exp(−d²/2) prior μ₀ = 0.1 + 0.4·v_frac m, σ₀ = 0.3 m, weight W₀ = 0.2 row mean μ = (W₀μ₀ + Σ wᵣ yᵣ) / (W₀ + Σ wᵣ) spread σ² = [W₀(σ₀² + (μ₀−μ)²) + Σ wᵣ (yᵣ − μ)²] / (W₀ + Σ wᵣ) + (σ₀ / (1 + n_eff))² risk P(amp > c) = 1 − Φ((c − μ)/σ) exploration 20% of moves: try a faster candidate whose risk ≤ 5%

7.8Important considerations and limits

  • Safety comes first, the model second. The model only chooses among moves inside the crane's rated envelope. If no candidate passes the risk limit, it falls back to the slowest move. In a real crane, the safety PLC and limit switches stay in charge.
  • Exploration is a risk. Trying faster moves is how the table learns, so it is capped at 5% predicted risk. On real hardware, run it in shadow mode first: predict, but let the existing anti-sway decide.
  • Sim-to-real gaps. Rigid ropes, a single plane, no load rotation, no hoisting during travel, viscous damping. Real data already differs: on the lab crane, every swing is slower than the logged cable length predicts (section 6). Next, the sim should add an effective-length offset. Then the claims must be shown on the lab data and, later, on an industrial crane's logs.
  • Real data is lab-scale. The public dataset is a small tower crane (cables under 1 m, payloads under 1 kg); we found no public industrial overhead-crane sway data.
  • Small-angle formulas are only for intuition. The simulation uses the full nonlinear equations above.
  • Out-of-distribution moves. n_eff (the summed kernel weight) and TabPFN's predictive spread both flag "nothing like this in the table". The decision rule turns that into caution automatically.
  • Licence. TabPFN-3.5 weights are non-commercial. That's fine for the hackathon; a product needs a commercial licence from Prior Labs.
  • What would falsify the pitch. If ZV tuned on T_meas matches TabPFN on phases 2–3, the honest claim shrinks to "risk-aware speed choice". We'd report that.
