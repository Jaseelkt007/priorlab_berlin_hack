Delta Pick Line

On food and pharma packaging lines, delta robots pick products off a conveyor with a suction cup and place them into trays more than 100 times a minute, at up to 15 g. At that speed only the suction cup holds the product, so every line trades speed against drops: run fast and products fall or land crooked; run slow and output is lost. Today that trade-off is set by hand, once per product, and conservatively. But products differ in ways a camera cannot see: weight, a porous or oily surface that leaks vacuum, an off-centre filling. So the robot is too careful with most picks, still fails on the odd ones, and every changeover means retuning. We use the sensor these grippers already have, the vacuum reading at the moment the cup touches. TabPFN predicts the drop risk of every possible speed from the line's own recent picks, with no training. The robot then takes the fastest safe speed for each pick and adapts within minutes when the product or conditions change.

2 · Why one pick fails

A suction cup has a fixed budget of force. Acceleration spends it.

Holding force = vacuum × cup area × efficiency. The product fails when the sideways inertia beats friction (shear), the vertical load beats the holding force (lift), or the tipping moment of a tall product beats the cup's grip at its edge (peel). The seal vacuum is the one input the robot can measure the moment it touches the product. Friction and centre of mass are hidden.

4 · How each pick is decided

Imagine every speed, pick the fastest safe one, learn from the outcome

Step 1 · cup touches (≈10 ms)

Read the seal

The vacuum sensor shows how well this particular product sealed. A leaky biscuit or a wrinkled film shows up here, before any acceleration.

Step 2 · one batched call

Ask TabPFN 15 questions

One row per candidate speed, 30% to 100%, with the outcome left blank. TabPFN reads the recent log of this product and returns a drop probability for each.

Step 3 · decide

Fastest speed under 0.5% risk

The risk limit is a business setting, changeable at any time without retraining. If no speed is safe, the product goes to a reject or manual lane.

Step 4 · after the place

Log the outcome

Dropped or not, plus the lowest vacuum during the move. A deep dip that still held is a near-miss: it teaches the limit without losing a product.

5 · Calibration

"0.5% risk" is only useful if it is true

Two meanings of calibration on this line

Calibration run. After a changeover the model has no rows for the new product, so its predictions are wide and it chooses slow, safe speeds. Within the first few dozen picks the near-miss signal (vacuum dips) pins down the limit and speed climbs. No engineer tunes anything.

Calibrated probabilities. When the model says "10% drop risk", about 1 in 10 such picks should really drop. We check this in shadow mode: the model predicts for every pick of every strategy, including the reckless ones, and we compare predictions with what happened. Points on the diagonal mean the numbers can be trusted for setting a risk limit.

Reliability diagram (all strategies, shadow mode)

Each dot is a group of picks with similar predicted risk. x: predicted, y: observed drop rate, bars: 90% interval from the count. Dot size grows with the number of picks.

8 · What we build and prove

From this page to a measured result

Simulation

MuJoCo delta cell

Delta kinematics with real limits (10 m/s, 150 m/s², jerk), conveyor and trays, suction modelled with MuJoCo's adhesion actuator plus friction, validated against the shear, lift and peel limits.

Data

Product catalogue

Biscuits, pouches, cheese, bars, blister cartons with realistic weight, seal and friction spread, plus humidity, cup wear and changeovers. The log uses the columns above.

Model

TabPFN-3.5

Classifier for drop, regressors for vacuum dip and placement error, cached context per product, batched over candidate speeds. Calibration checked in shadow mode.

Proof

Baselines and metrics

Fixed recipes, a GOMP-ST-style learned limit, an MLP on the same rows. Good packs per minute, drop rate, picks to recover after changeover, calibration error, decision latency.
