Push, Predict, Adapt

A world model answers one question before the robot moves: if I do this now, what will happen, and how sure am I?

TabPFN learns that answer from a small table of the robot's own past attempts, its log, with no training. The robot then uses the answer to choose a motion, or to check a motion some other controller proposed.

1 · The term

What a "world model" is, and why ours is a table

A world model is any model that predicts what happens next if an action is taken now. The world models you have seen predict the next video frame. Ours predicts the next numbers: slip, drop risk, where an object ends up. Same idea, different currency.

3 · How it plugs into a robot

Imagine many, do one. Two jobs. Any arm.

Imagine many, do one

"Asking 136 questions" means writing 136 what-if rows in software and sending them to TabPFN in one call. The arm does not move during this. Like a pool player picturing shots before taking one, the robot then executes one motion and logs one row.

imagine 6–136→do 1→log 1

Job 1: planner

The world model picks the motion itself: speed, grip, push direction. Use this when no other controller exists, or to tune one parameter (like speed) that a fixed program leaves open.

Job 2: safety check

Another controller (RL policy, VLA, human operator) proposes a motion. The world model predicts its outcome first: go, adjust, or send to a manual lane. A risk screen, not a certified safety system.

4 · Your questions, answered directly

The doubts worth settling first

What exactly is a "world model" here?

A model of what happens next if the robot does X now. Video world models answer in pixels. Ours answers in the numbers the robot logs: slip, drop risk, where the object ends up.

Where does the table come from?

The robot's own log. Every attempt is one row. Inputs come from sensors the arm already has: camera (box size), joint torques in the first 2 cm of a lift (how heavy it feels), gripper force, commanded speed. Outputs are measured afterwards: slip, drop, placement error.

Does it apply to the conveyor case with a heavy box?

Yes, that is the clearest application. A policy sees a normal-looking box and does its usual fast lift, so the heavy box slips out. Our robot does a 2 cm test lift, reads "feels 3.8× heavier", asks TabPFN how each speed and grip would go, and picks the fastest safe one.

"136 questions": doesn't the robot have to do the work?

It does, once. The questions are imagined in software in one batched call, while the arm holds still. Then the arm executes the single best motion and logs the result.

Is it a safety layer?

It can be, and that is one of its two jobs. It can choose the motion itself (planner), or screen motions proposed by an RL policy, a VLA or a person (safety check). It is a risk screen, never a certified safety system.

Does it depend on the arm brand?

No. The world model works in task terms (centimetres, newtons, speed). The arm's own controller turns the chosen motion into joint angles. Franka, UR, Kinova or SO-101 all work, and the model even absorbs a sloppy arm's quirks.

How does it adapt to something heavier than it has seen?

The test lift turns hidden weight into a number. TabPFN predicts from the rows nearest to that situation, with a wide spread if the rows are few, so the planner goes cautious. After the move, the outcome is a new row and the next prediction uses it. No weights change. If no available motion is safe, it says so: manual lane.

Can't a well-trained RL policy or VLA handle heavy boxes?

Within its training, yes. RL trained on randomized weights, with memory of recent steps, adapts inside that range. Outside it, or for a property nobody randomized, it needs new training. We must prove our advantage against exactly that strong baseline.

What we have to prove by Sunday 16:30

Claims we will measure, not assume

Demo options (MuJoCo, Franka)

  • A · Pushing: easiest physics to get right; build first.
  • B · Conveyor lift: the strongest real-world story; needs reliable grip-slip physics, so we test it in a short spike before committing.
  • Same world-model and planner code for both.

Hypotheses

  • On objects outside RL's training range, TabPFN succeeds with fewer attempts.
  • Its 90% prediction bands contain about 90% of real outcomes.
  • As a safety check, it prevents most drops of a fast policy.

Baselines and tests

  • PPO on one object; PPO with randomized physics and history, trained fast in JAX/MJX
  • MLP-ensemble world model given the same rows
  • Held-out objects, hundreds of seeded episodes, decision latency
